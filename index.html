03 · WHY IT MATTERS

The coin rotation paradox

Every time the rolling gear gets back to where it started, it has spun on its own axis 37 times — not the number of teeth divided by the ring's, the way you'd guess. Turn the dial all the way to 24, so the rolling gear matches the ring tooth for tooth, and you get the classic result: roll one coin once around an identical coin and it flips twice, not once. Going around isn't the same as spinning — count it yourself with two coins on a table if it doesn't look right.

Why clock gears look like this

Almost every gear you've ever seen — car, bicycle, kitchen mixer — uses an involute tooth profile: cheap to cut, and it meshes well across a wide range of tooth counts. Traditional clock and watch gearing doesn't. It uses a cycloidal profile instead — the tooth face shaped like an epicycloid, the flank like a hypocycloid — because clock pinions run at very low tooth counts, sometimes single digits, and involute teeth mesh badly down there. The curve this page draws is the same family of curve cut into every mechanical clock gear you've ever taken apart.

Not the same curve

Two things share a name with this curve closely enough to get confused with it, so: the rotor housing in a Wankel engine is an epitrochoid — a related curve where the pen sits off the rolling gear's rim rather than on it — not an epicycloid; the two look similar but aren't the same shape. And an epicyclic (planetary) gear train is a mechanical layout where gears' axes orbit each other — it shares a word root with the tooth profile drawn here and nothing else.
